ARGO: cutting a 21-ft canoe mold in-house with a hot wire

For the 2026 ASCE competition our team switched to a wide, tumblehome hull, and stopped outsourcing the mold. On the mold sub-team I helped turn the CAD hull into a foam mold with a homemade hot-wire cutter, designed it to come apart after casting, and helped cast and demold the boat.

Challenge

ARGO's tumblehome hull curves inward above the waterline. That lets paddlers take more vertical strokes and makes the boat more stable, but it creates a molding problem: the bottom of the boat is wider than the gunwales. In past years, concrete shrank onto the mold during curing and the mold had to be destroyed to get the boat out. This boat was also bigger than any before it. On top of that, the team wanted to stop paying an outside shop to CNC the foam.

32 inMax beam (tumblehome)
5/8 inHull wall thickness

Mold process

  1. Slice the CAD. We took hull cross-sections from Fusion 360 every 12 in. Each one kept its offset from the gunwale line to the stern axis, so we could cast directly on the mold without shimming it.
  2. Add the wall. We added a 5/8 in edge from the gunwale to that axis on every section. It doubles as a thickness guide at the edges.
  3. Laser-cut templates. We cut each section from 1/4 in plywood, which is stiff enough that the hot wire doesn't burn straight through it.
  4. Stack and cut. We glued insulation-foam boards into 12 in blocks, fixed a template to each face, and traced both outlines with the hot wire. Each block becomes a loft between two stations.
  5. End caps. We rough-cut the 32 in end caps from Styrofoam with the hot wire, then hand-sanded them to shape. The end caps stay in the boat after casting.
  6. Assemble. We pinned each section to the casting table with two diagonal dowels in custom 3D-printed holders, filled the gaps with spray foam, sanded the transitions smooth, and wrapped the mold in a stretchy fabric release layer.

Designing for demolding: the "Core Four"

The four center sections have the largest overhang, so they would trap the boat. We gave each of these "Core Four" pieces an extra T-shaped cut that splits it into three parts. To demold, you pull the center T first. The two side pieces then fold inward and slide out, and the remaining sections slide toward the middle and come out one at a time. The boat came off cleanly, and most of the foam can be reused.

Cast day & demolding

We cast ARGO on January 24, 2026, on schedule. The mold team hand-placed concrete as the mix team delivered about two batches every ten minutes:

  • First layer, 3/8 in: started four feet from the bow and worked in both directions. Rubber straps of known 3/8 in thickness, laid every 12 in, served as depth references.
  • Reinforcement: SpiderLath fiberglass mesh, laid 24 in at a time with 2 in overlaps. On the overhangs, two people pushed concrete into the mold from each side.
  • Second layer, 1/4 in: checked with marked toothpicks in every 4 in square, then floated smooth to cut down on sanding.
  • Cure: wrapped in plastic inside the hydration tent, kept above 85% humidity for three weeks.

Results

Outcome

The in-house hot-wire process took about three fewer weeks and several thousand fewer dollars than outsourcing CNC work, and every new mold member learned CAD along the way. The hot wire was a little less precise than a CNC machine, so we made up the difference by hand-sanding to shape. The Core Four design let us demold without destroying the mold, which had not been possible on previous boats.

  • Our analysis predicted a four-paddler midship freeboard of about 7.3 in, comfortably above the 6 in minimum. The FEA run agreed closely.
  • We competed at the 2026 ASCE Upstate NY Student Symposium at RIT (April 9–11).

2026–27: in progress

● In progress · updated Oct 2026

This season's work builds directly on what ARGO taught us.

  • Hull CFD. I'm running hull CFD in ANSYS Fluent to compare revisions of this year's ~20 ft hull before the geometry is locked.
  • A mold that survives several pours. We're reworking the foam mold process so one mold holds up across practice casts and the final cast. That means tighter casting tolerances and less patchwork between pours.
  • Turning constraints into geometry. I'm translating mix sub-team test results, new competition rules (including a thinner wall), and shop machining limits into specific hull and mold revisions.
  • Better tooling. The team is building a larger, stiffer hot-wire cutter and 3D-printed depth gauges for casting.

Princeton Carbon Works · Summer 2026

Parametric rim aerodynamics for triathlon wheels

Deeper rims cut drag. That part is obvious. The harder question is which details of the cross-section matter once depth is fixed. I built a four-parameter rim model, ran full yaw sweeps in CFD on every candidate, and weighted the results by how long a triathlete actually spends at each yaw angle.

Problem

Wheel brands usually optimize a rim shape at 0° yaw, with the wind straight on. Real riders almost never see that. Crosswinds put a triathlete at a spread of yaw angles, and the shape that wins head-on is often not the one that wins over a whole race. Princeton Carbon Works wanted to know which geometric features drive drag on their deep triathlon rims, beyond the obvious answer of "go deeper."

Parametric model

My first iterations borrowed from NACA airfoil theory. I traced elliptical profiles that matched the leading and trailing edges to the tire curvature and moved max width between 30 and 50% of depth. Those early shapes mostly confirmed that narrow is aero, and they converged near the industry-standard rim width of about 105% of tire width. To get past that, I rebuilt the profile as a spline controlled by four parameters, so every candidate was a single named point in the design space:

Schematic only, not to scale. Each candidate was named by its four values, e.g. P1_P2_P3_P4, so results could be sorted and compared directly.

  • P1, max-width position: where the widest point sits along the depth.
  • P2, max rim width: as a percentage of tire width. This sets how cleanly flow transitions from tire to rim.
  • P3, shoulder (vertical) spline handle: how quickly the profile sheds width after its widest point.
  • P4, trailing-edge spline handle: the shape of the convergence at the spoke bed.

CFD & yaw weighting

  1. Model. I built each 75 and 95 mm candidate in SolidWorks and Fusion 360 from the four parameters, on a 28 mm tire.
  2. Simulate. I ran a full yaw-range sweep from 0° to 20° on every candidate in NablaFlow AeroCloud, comparing drag force at each angle.
  3. Weight. I wrote a Python script that bins drag into eight yaw bins and weights each bin by the share of race time a triathlete spends there. I computed the weighting two ways, nearest-neighbor and interpolation, to check that the result did not depend on the method.
  4. Rank. I sorted the weighted drag across folders of candidates (exploratory sets, 75 mm, 95 mm, and disc) and tracked how much each parameter moved the result.

Findings

Key result

Max rim width (P2) was the dominant parameter, with an optimum near 115% of tire width at both depths. That is wider than the common ~105% standard. Once the shoulder geometry was tuned, the wider rim held flow attached at the tire-to-rim junction across more of the yaw range.

  • 0° optimization is unreliable. Shapes that were fastest head-on were often not fastest after yaw weighting. High-yaw performance decided the ranking.
  • Depth changes which parameters matter. At 75 mm, P2 led clearly. At 95 mm, the shoulder handle (P3) and max-width position (P1) together carried as much influence as width. With more chord behind the widest point, the flow needs more room to recover pressure. A designer moving to deeper rims who only tunes width will miss the biggest gains.
  • A shorter shoulder was always better. The shortest P3 tested won at both depths, so the optimum may lie lower still. The real limit is probably manufacturing or structure, not aerodynamics.
  • The trailing edge is "set and hold." P4 had a stable optimum that did not move with depth. Once the other three parameters are set, it barely needs revisiting.
  • Balance beats extremes. A good pairing of side and bottom spline lengths outperformed letting either one dominate.

Exact drag values and candidate geometries are Princeton Carbon Works' data and are left out on purpose. I'm happy to walk through the method in an interview.

What I learned

  • How to design a parameter study so the results answer a question, instead of producing a pile of runs.
  • Why the weighting scheme matters as much as the solver. The "best" shape changed once I weighted for real conditions.
  • How to turn CFD output into a ranked, defensible recommendation for an engineering team.

Next steps I proposed: test shoulder handles shorter than the current minimum against structural limits, and add trispoke-equipped runs. No trispoke data existed yet for any candidate.

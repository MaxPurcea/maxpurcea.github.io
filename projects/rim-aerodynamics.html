Princeton Carbon Works · Summer 2026

Parametric Rim Aerodynamics

Parametric CAD and race-weighted CFD used to redesign a 95 mm triathlon rim, reducing its simulated race drag by about 36% compared with the baseline profile.

Objective

Decrease the effect of drag on a 95 mm triathlon rim across the crosswind angles riders see in a race.

Contribution

Built a parametric rim model to improve the efficiency and accuracy of repeated CFD yaw sweeps. Utilized Python to scale yaw influence over race conditions and analyze which parameters impacted drag at each depth.

Result

The final 95 mm profile cut race-weighted drag by about 36%. Ranked profiles and design guidelines for 75 and 95 mm rims went to the engineering team.

Results

Over the summer the 95 mm rim went through several rounds of redesign, and each round lowered race-weighted drag. Narrowing the rim gave the first large gain. Setting the maximum width at 105% of tire width and moving it to 40% of rim depth gave the second. Tuning the shoulder and trailing-edge splines brought the total reduction to about 36%.

Most of that gain comes in crosswind. Head-on, the final profile has about 19% less drag than the baseline. At 5° the difference is 36%, and by 10° the new profile turns its side force into net forward thrust, much like a sail. Riders spend most of a race between 2.5° and 7.5° of yaw, which is why designing for those angles paid off.

Process

  1. Early profiles were elliptical shapes based on NACA airfoil theory. To search the design space more systematically, the rim cross-section was rebuilt in SolidWorks and Fusion 360 as a spline controlled by four parameters: maximum width, the position of maximum width, shoulder length, and trailing-edge length. All profiles were modeled on a 28 mm tire.
  2. Each profile was simulated in NablaFlow AeroCloud across a yaw sweep from 0° to 20°. More than 100 profiles were simulated at 65, 75, and 95 mm depths.
  3. A Python script weighted the drag at each yaw angle by the share of race time triathletes spend there, so each design was scored on the drag it would see over a full race. The weighting was computed by both nearest-neighbor and interpolation to confirm the rankings did not depend on the method.
  4. Results from the 75 and 95 mm data sets were compared to find which parameters drove drag at each depth, then summarized as design guidelines for the engineering team.

Key findings

  • Head-on, drag kept falling as rims got wider, down to about 115% of tire width. Once weighted across race yaw angles, the best profiles were narrower, at 100–105%, because they held up better in crosswind. Even within the final set, the profile with the lowest drag at 0° was not the fastest over a race.
  • Placing maximum width near 40% of rim depth performed best at both depths, and that placement carried into the final 95 mm profile.
  • Which parameters mattered most changed with depth. Maximum width had the largest effect on the 75 mm rim. On the 95 mm rim, shoulder length and the position of maximum width mattered as much or more, because the longer section behind the widest point needs more room to recover pressure.
  • The trailing edge had the same optimum at both depths, so it can be set early. Balanced side and bottom spline lengths outperformed profiles dominated by either one.

All results are from CFD on a rim-and-tire model and are shown relative to the baseline profile. Absolute values and final geometries are proprietary to Princeton Carbon Works.

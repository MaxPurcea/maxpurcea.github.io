Princeton Carbon Works · Summer 2026

GD&T frame drawings and derailleur hanger replicas

Two projects about making parts fit. The first was manufacturing drawings that tell a carbon frame supplier exactly which dimensions matter. The second was caliper-measured replicas of the two industry-standard derailleur hangers, 3D printed and fitted to a prototype bike.

Manufacturing drawings for a carbon frame

I made 2D SolidWorks drawings that call out the specifications every add-on component depends on. That way the brake, drivetrain, and cockpit parts fit the frame the first time.

  • Datum strategy. I referenced dimensions to the features everything else hangs off of: the bottom bracket and the rear dropouts.
  • Tolerances where they matter. I held critical interfaces like brake mounting holes tight and left depth and size freedom on less important features, using GD&T to state that intent clearly.
  • Designing for the process. I learned that fork and rear dropouts can flex during curing, so those features got deliberately looser tolerances than a machined part would.

Reverse-engineering the Shimano DH and SRAM UDH

Before designing its own hanger for an upcoming road and track bike, the team needed accurate models of the two standards it would have to interface with. Working only from calipers, a protractor, and hand sketches, I modeled both the Shimano direct-mount hanger and SRAM's Universal Derailleur Hanger (UDH) in SolidWorks.

Result

I 3D printed both replicas. They interfaced correctly with a real frame and derailleur, so they could be used on the prototype bike in development.

What I learned

  • A drawing is a contract. Every tolerance costs money, so it should be there for a reason.
  • Composite parts move during manufacturing, and the drawing has to account for that.
  • Careful measurement and a few test prints beat guessing at a spec.
